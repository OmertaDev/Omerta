# THE PILLARS — marketing pack

**The gap this fills.** `docs/OMR-MARKETING-PACK.md` sells the token. `docs/GAMEPLAY-MARKETING-PACK.md`
sells the character build. Between them they cover two of the seven pillars in `MARKETING.md` §2.
This is the other five, plus the two antagonist arcs — written as chapters you can post one at a
time, in any order, without any of them depending on the others.

**Every number here is measured from `src/rules.js`, not remembered.** Where a figure is a founder
sign-off lever it is still the live value; if a lever moves, this file is stale and the post is
wrong. Re-measure before a campaign.

**The claim discipline is unchanged and binds every line below.** No earnings claims, no token-price
claims, no mint-scarcity framing, never "anyone can cash out real stocks", and referrals are a flat
one-time cash finder's fee. Anything not live is stated in the tense `MARKETING.md` §8's table
gives it.

## Chapter 1 — THE FAMILY

**The promise.** Most games call a guild a chat room with a shared bank. This one makes it a
government with a treasury, a map, five named jobs, and an election.

**Master line:** *Twenty men, six districts, and one chair that decides what the whole city plays
under this week.*

**System line:** Found a family at level 5 for $25,000. Twenty made men, maximum. The turf is real
estate — six core districts, each with a perk that runs while you hold it — and it changes hands
two ways: a **sealed bid**, where every family commits a secret stake for 30 minutes and the loser
forfeits half of what they put up, or a **war**, which costs $10,000 to declare, runs 30 minutes,
pays the winner 20% of the loser's treasury, and scores a fire-kill at 3 points against a jump's 1.

**The Watch is the part nobody expects.** A family that holds a district declares a four-hour window
each day when it stands ready. Take that ground *outside* the window and it costs 1.5× more.
Declaring is free and public — so the map carries every family's stated bedtime, and the whole
question becomes when you can be there rather than what you can afford.

**Five posts, one man each.** The Enforcer makes your turf dearer to contest. The Caporegime slows
Bureau scrutiny on your operations. The Streetboss cuts the war chest. The Quartermaster arms your
convoys. The Bagman makes the operations pad cheaper. A post counts **only while its holder is
alive, out of lockup and out of the hospital** — so putting a rival's Enforcer in a cell makes their
turf cheaper to take without touching the district at all. Reassignment sits on the **man**, not the
chair, on a six-hour cooldown, so a family whose officer goes down spends a man off the street
rather than sliding one across.

### The thread

> 1/ Your guild in most games is a chat room with a shared wallet.
>
> 2/ Here it's a government. Twenty made men. Six districts. A treasury other families can take 20%
> of by winning a war.
>
> 3/ Turf changes hands by sealed bid. Everyone commits in secret, 30 minutes, highest takes it —
> and losers forfeit half their stake. Over-committing against a family that was never coming costs
> real money.
>
> 4/ The holder declares a 4-hour Watch each day. Attack outside it and you pay 1.5×. It's public.
> The map literally shows you when every family sleeps.
>
> 5/ Five officer posts. One man each. They only count while he's alive, free and out of the
> hospital.
>
> 6/ Which means the way to make a family's turf cheap isn't to outbid them. It's to put their
> Enforcer in a cell first.

### Headlines
- *The map shows you when they sleep.*
- *One man per chair. Put him in a cell and the chair is empty.*
- *A sealed bid where losing still costs you.*

### Caption
> Six districts. Twenty men. Five jobs that only count while the man holding them is standing.

## Chapter 2 — THE COMMISSION

**The promise.** A weekly election, run by the five biggest families, whose result changes the rules
for every player on the server — including everyone who did not vote.

**Master line:** *Five seats. One vote a week. The whole city plays under whatever they pass.*

**System line:** The top five families by seasonal standing hold seats. Each seat's boss or
underboss casts one public, changeable vote a week. The majority of *last* week's votes governs
*this* week. A tie deadlocks and nothing passes.

**The eight decrees, and each one touches exactly one thing:**

| Decree | What changes |
|---|---|
| **Open season** | Safehouses cost half — hiding gets cheap, so more people take the risk |
| **Pax** | No new wars may be declared. Existing ones run on |
| **Amnesty** | Laying low costs half |
| **Lockdown** | Convoy guards defend harder |
| **Smuggler's Moon** | The Coast Guard interdicts less |
| **Open Roads** | Convoys arrive faster |
| **Blood Oath** | A fire-kill loots more cash |
| **The Levy** | The 12-hour buyback's family split goes to the seated chamber instead of the standing top |

**And there is a veto.** The head seat's boss — only the boss, not the underboss — can kill the
decree in force, once a week, in public. Then a coalition of seated families can **override** it if
they can muster the weight. Motions cost a **$100,000 treasury deposit**; the enacted one is
refunded and the rest is forfeit, so putting a motion on the ballot is a bet.

**The standing.** Voting, proposing, overriding, vetoing and getting a motion enacted all pay
statecraft — an account-level record that survives death, ranked from Ward Heeler to Il Capo di
Tutti Capi. Governing is a career, and the ladder remembers it.

### The thread

> 1/ Five families hold seats on the Commission. Seats are earned by this season's standing, not
> bought and not permanent.
>
> 2/ Each seat votes once a week, in public, and can change its vote all week.
>
> 3/ Last week's majority governs this week. A tie deadlocks — nothing passes. Silence is a result.
>
> 4/ Eight decrees. Half-price safehouses. A ban on new wars. Cheaper laying low. Better convoy
> guards. And "Blood Oath", which just makes killing people pay more.
>
> 5/ Every player on the server plays under it, including the ones who never voted.
>
> 6/ The head seat's boss can veto once a week. A coalition can override the veto. Putting a motion
> up costs $100,000 of your family's treasury, and only the one that passes gets it back.
>
> 7/ It is, functionally, a legislature. In a mafia game. With a veto.

### Headlines
- *Five families vote. Everyone lives under it.*
- *There is a veto. There is also an override.*
- *A tie deadlocks — silence is a result.*

## Chapter 3 — VICE

**The promise.** A real casino with a published edge and a verifiable draw, not a slot machine
skinned as a game mechanic.

**Master line:** *The house edge is published. So is the draw.*

**System line:** Every game in the Den is **cash-denominated — the house never takes $OMR.** Table
limits run $100 to $250,000; a level-30 player gets the high-stakes room at $2,000,000. The games
are the real ones with the real edges: **blackjack** pays 3:2, dealer stands on 17 and hits soft 17;
**craps** resolves the whole pass line in one call at 1:1; **the Numbers** pays the historically
accurate 600:1, which is a ~40% edge and we say so.

**The Numbers has a near-miss tier and a jackpot.** Land within ±5 of the draw and you take 5× your
stake. Half a percent of every stake feeds a progressive pot, and an exact hit takes half of it on
top of the 600:1 — the rest rolls, so the marquee never restarts from zero.

**The draw is provable.** Every day the game publishes a **sealed commitment** — a hash of tomorrow's
result plus a one-way nonce — before a single ticket is sold. The next day it publishes the nonce
and the result, so anyone can recompute the hash and confirm the number was fixed before the money
went down. The board publishes the exact formula. If a stored commitment ever fails to recompute,
the board says so rather than hiding it.

**And there's a whole racing card underneath it.** Six runners a race, posted decimal odds carrying a
uniform 15% takeout, two slots per card reserved for **player-owned animals** — buy a greyhound or a
racehorse, train it, and run it in the card the entire town is betting on. Bets are fixed-odds:
locked on your ticket at the moment you place it, so the board moves as runners enter and your price
does not.

**Plus the parimutuel layer:** a poker tournament at $5,000 a seat paying 50/30/20 net of a 5% rake,
and the Futurity — nominate your own animal for $5,000, the town backs the field, winners split the
losing pool.

### The thread

> 1/ Most "casinos" in games are a button that takes your token and sometimes gives more back.
>
> 2/ Ours is a casino. Blackjack pays 3:2, dealer hits soft 17. Craps pays the pass line at 1:1.
> The Numbers pays 600:1, which is a 40% house edge, and we print that number.
>
> 3/ Every game is cash. The house does not take the token.
>
> 4/ The daily draw is committed in public *before* tickets are sold: a hash of the result and a
> one-way nonce.
>
> 5/ Next day we publish the nonce. You recompute the hash yourself. If the house moved the number
> after seeing the bets, the arithmetic breaks in your hands.
>
> 6/ We also publish the formula. And if a stored commitment ever fails to verify, the board says
> so instead of hiding it.
>
> 7/ You can also buy a greyhound, train it, and enter it in the daily card the whole town bets on.

### Headlines
- *We publish the edge. Then we publish the draw.*
- *600:1. That's a 40% house edge. We're telling you.*
- *Buy the dog. Train the dog. Let the city bet on your dog.*

### Honest caveat to carry
Gambling in this game is play-money cash, not the token, and the house is a net sink by design. Do
not frame any of it as a way to make money.

## Chapter 4 — THE BUREAU

**The promise.** A state that builds a case against you slowly, from things you actually did, and
that you can see coming for a long time before it arrives.

**Master line:** *Heat is weather. The case is a file, and the file remembers.*

**System line:** Heat spikes and decays. Underneath it, a second number — **exposure** — banks
slowly from sustained heat and does not forget. Four stages, published: **clean → watched at 200 →
under investigation at 1,000 → indicted at 3,000.**

**Two ways out before the file is filed.** A **bribe** — wealth-scaled, minimum $25,000 — knocks 800
off the meter. A **lawyer's retainer** at $150,000 buys three days during which a conviction is
40% less likely and forfeiture 30% smaller. And the **envelope**: 90 $OMR keeps a standing wire to
the precinct for a week, halving how fast the case builds *and* doubling how fast it bleeds. That
one is proactive — you pay it before there is anything to fix.

**Then the indictment lands, and it is a latch.** You get a six-hour grace window and three choices:
**plea** (a certain 15% of pocket and bank, plus a short stretch), **buy the jury** for 120 $OMR
(which halves the conviction odds, once), or **demand trial**. Conviction seizes **30% of pocket and
bank** — and **staked $OMR and minted gear are safe**, because the Law is an economic antagonist,
not death. Your street survives.

**If you don't show up, the worker comes for you anyway.** Past the grace window the game force-busts
you offline, which is the point: hiding is not a strategy.

**Or you flip.** Name a rival, drop your own case, seed 1,500 exposure onto theirs, and take a
permanent **rat** brand that follows your bloodline — after which your family's omertà no longer
protects you and anyone can put a price on your head. Two days of witness protection is what you get
in exchange.

### The thread

> 1/ Heat in most games is a bar that goes down when you wait.
>
> 2/ Here heat is the weather. Underneath it there's a second number that banks slowly and doesn't
> forget: exposure.
>
> 3/ Four stages and we publish the thresholds. Clean. Watched at 200. Under investigation at 1,000.
> Indicted at 3,000.
>
> 4/ Before the file is filed you can bribe it down, or put a lawyer on retainer for three days, or
> keep a standing envelope going — which halves how fast the case builds and doubles how fast it
> cools.
>
> 5/ After it's filed: plea to a certain loss, buy the jury once, or demand trial. Conviction takes
> 30% of your cash. Your staked tokens and your on-chain gear are untouched.
>
> 6/ Not logging in doesn't help. Past the grace window a background job convicts you in absentia.
>
> 7/ Or flip. Name a rival, seed 1,500 exposure onto them, take a permanent RAT brand your heir
> inherits, and lose your own family's protection forever.
>
> 8/ It's an antagonist arc. Most games don't have one.

### Headlines
- *The meter is public. So are all four thresholds.*
- *Not logging in is not a defence.*
- *You can flip. Your grandchildren will still be a rat.*

## Chapter 5 — THE PEN

**The promise.** Jail is a place with its own economy, its own politics and two ways out, not a
timer you stare at.

**Master line:** *A sentence is a location, and there is a wall.*

**System line:** Work the yard — 15 energy shaves a minute off your stretch and pays. Buy protection
from the yard boss, $15,000 plus half a percent of your net worth, for two hours nobody can shank
you — and while it holds **you can't shank anybody either**, because a shield is not a bunker. Bribe
the guard at $200 a second for a straight cash exit.

**Or take the shiv.** Both men must be inside. A shank costs 25 energy, lands about half the time
against an even mark, and **bypasses every street defence** — a safehouse can't be entered from a
cell and a street bodyguard isn't in the room — while still respecting pre-paid revive insurance and
witness protection. It kills, with an heir and a sworn feud, but it takes no loot and pays no
reputation, because a shanking is dishonourable. Land it and your own sentence grows by ten minutes.
Get caught and it's thirty minutes in the hole, where you can neither act nor be touched.

**Four yard crews.** Run with one and every live crewmate in the block makes you harder to shank, up
to a cap, with an extra shield for the most feared man in the crew — who is the shot-caller, computed
live, never appointed. You cannot shank your own crew.

**And there is the wall.** Buy a hacksaw and rope for $50,000, roll about a third, and you walk —
straight into **two days as a WANTED fugitive**, which strips your family's protection and puts NPC
bounty hunters on you. Or plan a **crew break**: two to four men, odds starting at 40% and climbing
12 points per extra body, capped at 90%. Everyone walks together, everyone walks out wanted.

**Any of them can be the rat.** One crewman tips the guards and the break blows regardless of the
roll — and the rat's deal is relief only. They dodge the added stretch and the beating; they do not
get out early. The feed never names them.

### The thread

> 1/ Jail in most games is a timer.
>
> 2/ Here it's a place. You can work the yard to shave your sentence. Buy protection from the yard
> boss. Bribe a guard by the second.
>
> 3/ You can also stab someone. Both of you have to be inside. It bypasses safehouses and bodyguards
> — you can't hide in a cell.
>
> 4/ It kills for real. Heir, sworn feud, the works. It takes no loot and pays no reputation,
> because a shanking is dishonourable.
>
> 5/ Four yard crews. Running with one makes you harder to hit, and the most feared man in the block
> is the shot-caller. Computed live. Nobody appoints him.
>
> 6/ Or go over the wall. $50,000 for a hacksaw, a 35% roll, and two days as a WANTED fugitive with
> hunters on you.
>
> 7/ Or plan a crew break. Four men gets you to 76%. Everyone walks together.
>
> 8/ Any one of them can rat. Then it blows no matter what you rolled — and the feed never says who.

### Headlines
- *A safehouse can't be entered from a cell.*
- *Four men, 76%, one of whom might be the rat.*
- *The most feared man in the block is the shot-caller. Nobody voted.*

## Chapter 6 — DEATH, AND WHAT SURVIVES IT

**The promise.** Death is real and it is not a reset. It costs the street everything and hands the
bloodline something.

**Master line:** *The man dies. The line remembers.*

**System line:** A player kill loots **25% of the victim's pocket cash**, **half of a loose $OMR
balance against a fifth of a committed one**, and has a **15% chance to strip a piece of in-game
gear** — while gear already taken on-chain is out of reach, which is the whole tradeoff: keep it in
play to use it, or extract it to keep it. (Taking gear on-chain is **built and not open** —
see Chapter 9's caveat.) A rookie under level 10 is worth nothing to hunt, which is
new-player protection without a rule.

**The Sacking is the sharpest mechanic in the game.** A player fire-kill lets the killer **take one
of the victim's business fronts** — the actual passive-income engine, chosen as the most valuable one
the killer can legally hold. Not the cash it made. The building.

**The estate then taxes what's left.** A quarter of the heir's inherited liquid $OMR burns. The
staked balance, the vault, the compound and the estate are untouched — the safe harbours stay safe,
so dying costs the bloodline something without touching what the game told you was protected.

**And it swears a vendetta.** A fire-kill puts your victim's bloodline against yours for seven days,
which their heir inherits at birth. Kill again and it escalates: Vendetta, then **Blood Feud** at 1.5×
the window, then **War of Extinction** at 2×, which will not simply lapse — you settle it or you sue
for peace at a sit-down. A revenge kill inside the window pays **double** feared reputation and
waives the floor on posting a named contract on them.

**The bloodline hall is the record.** Every generation, its epithet, its cause of death, and a
dynasty score. That, the prestige, the collection, the compound and the vault all survive.

**The defences are real and all of them cost.** A safehouse is $25,000 for four hours — but it's a
shield, not a bunker: while you're under it you cannot hit, deal, launder or collect, and you get
twelve hours of it a day, total. A bodyguard absorbs exactly one lethal blow and is hospitalised in
your place. Pre-paid revive insurance absorbs one. The earned shield burns before the paid one.

### The thread

> 1/ You can die permanently. Your heir picks up the account.
>
> 2/ A killer takes a quarter of your pocket cash, half your loose tokens — a fifth if you'd
> committed them — and has a 15% shot at a piece of your gear.
>
> 3/ Gear you'd already taken on-chain is untouchable. That's the trade: keep it in play to use it,
> or extract it to keep it.
>
> 4/ And they can take one of your businesses. Not its income. The building. Off your corpse.
>
> 5/ Your heir inherits the estate, the compound, the vault, the prestige and the collection. And a
> vendetta, sworn against the line that did it, that they were born into.
>
> 6/ Kill the same bloodline twice and it escalates to a Blood Feud. Three times and it's a War of
> Extinction, which doesn't lapse. You settle it or you sue for peace.
>
> 7/ Revenge inside the window pays double.
>
> 8/ Under level 10 you're worth nothing to hunt. New player protection with no rule attached.

### Headlines
- *They didn't take the money. They took the building.*
- *Your heir is born owing somebody.*
- *A War of Extinction does not lapse. You settle it or you sit down.*

## Chapter 7 — THE LIVING WORLD

**The promise.** The city moves whether or not you are logged in, on a schedule you can read a week
ahead.

**Master line:** *The city has weather, a calendar, and five cartels that fight back.*

**System line:** Five NPC outfits hold the map's frontier: the **Dock Rats** at level 4, the **Zappa
Crew** at 8 — both soloable — and then **Kryl**, **Moreau** and **Volkov** at levels 20, 40 and 55,
which are **crew-only** and cannot be soloed at all. Each is a shared reservoir the whole server
grinds down together, so beating one is co-operative rather than competitive. A raid costs energy,
ammunition and heat, sits on a two-hour cooldown, and takes a capped slice.

**Rout one and it gets angry.** A routed outfit defends far harder for three hours — so the reservoir
cannot be farmed to the floor over and over, and the counter is scheduling rather than damage.

**They also come for you.** An outfit can open a hostility against a *player family* unprompted and
send men on a three-hour cadence. Every earned shield still applies at resolve, the family is told
who to hit back, and routing the outfit ends its campaign — or you can sue for peace through the
ordinary pact route, which stops the guns.

**Seasons twist the rules for 28 days.** Three phases — the opening, the long game, and **the
Reckoning**, which escalates the last week: turf a quarter cheaper to challenge, contests settling in
half the time, watch windows halved. And a seed-drawn modifier the whole server plays under: **The
Crackdown** (the Law builds 25% faster, laying low is a quarter cheaper), **Blood in the Streets**
(kills loot 15% more, safehouses cost 25% more), **The Gold Rush**, or **Dead Quiet** — vanilla.

**And there is one hour a night.** Prime Time is a rotating UTC hour, forecast a week out, with a
drawn mechanic and a drawn mode. **The Rally**: answer the call and the reward scales with how many
people showed, settled at the window's close so nobody is punished for arriving early. **Happy Hour**:
the house buys rounds. **The Siege**: a shared damage bar that needs eight people to crack, and only
a cracked siege pays anyone.

### The thread

> 1/ Five cartels hold the frontier. Two you can take alone. Three you cannot — Kryl, Moreau and
> Volkov are crew-only, hard-gated.
>
> 2/ Each is a shared pool the whole server grinds down together. Beating one is co-operative.
>
> 3/ Rout one and it comes back enraged for three hours, defending much harder. You can't just farm
> it to the floor.
>
> 4/ They also declare on player families unprompted and send men every few hours until you rout
> them — or sue for peace.
>
> 5/ Seasons run 28 days in three phases. The last week is the Reckoning: turf a quarter cheaper,
> contests half as long, watch windows halved.
>
> 6/ Each season draws a modifier. The Crackdown makes the Law 25% faster. Blood in the Streets makes
> kills loot 15% more and hiding 25% dearer.
>
> 7/ And one hour a night — forecast a week ahead — the whole city is in the same room. Sometimes
> it's a rally whose payout scales with turnout. Sometimes it's a siege that needs eight people or
> nobody gets paid.

### Headlines
- *Three of the five cartels cannot be soloed. At all.*
- *Rout it and it comes back angry.*
- *One hour a night, forecast a week out, and a siege that needs eight of you.*

## Chapter 8 — THE KITCHEN

**The promise.** A production chain with a workforce that gets paid whether or not you sold anything.

**Master line:** *The crew draws wages while the stash sits.*

**System line:** Eight products, from cheap street-grade to the top shelf — a hundredfold spread in
what a unit is worth. Five labs, from a **$20,000 bathtub rig** holding twenty units to **The
Cathedral** at $10,000,000 plus 1,200 $OMR holding four hundred. Cook, wait, collect: purity decides
quality, the lab decides the batch cap, and quality decides what a corner will pay.

**Then the decision that makes it a business.** **Cut it** — $8,000 buys 40% more units at 15% less
quality, floored so it can never become worthless. More product, worse product. That is the trade,
and it is yours to make.

**The crew is the sharp edge.** Hire up to five hands and they sell while you're offline. Each one
draws **$1,200 an hour, forever, whether or not the stash moved**, and each hire costs $50,000 more
than the last. Wages accrue up to two days. **Three days unpaid and they down tools** — the offline
sales stop dead and the stash sits until you square up. There is a door out: fire one, or let a cold
crew walk for nothing.

**And the Bureau is watching the corner, not the lab.** Dealing draws heat; a heat spike past the
threshold rolls a raid on the accrued minutes. Income-only fronts never get raided — the risk tracks
the extraction, not the earning.

### The thread

> 1/ Eight products. Five labs, from a $20,000 bathtub rig to a $10M cathedral.
>
> 2/ Cook, wait, collect. Purity decides quality, quality decides what the corner pays.
>
> 3/ Then the actual decision: cut it. $8,000 for 40% more units at 15% less quality. More product,
> worse product.
>
> 4/ Hire a crew and they sell while you're logged out. Up to five hands.
>
> 5/ They draw $1,200 an hour each. Whether or not the stash moved. Whether or not you logged in.
>
> 6/ Three days unpaid and they walk off the job. Sales stop. The stash sits there until you pay the
> nut.
>
> 7/ Every earner in this game has a bill attached. That's the design.

### Headlines
- *They get paid whether or not you sold anything.*
- *More product, worse product. Pick.*
- *Three days late and the corner goes quiet.*

## Chapter 9 — STREET DEEDS

**The promise.** Name a street, own it forever, and let somebody else fight you for what it earns.

**Master line:** *You own the paper. Control is something else entirely.*

**System line:** Claim one named street — three to twenty-eight characters, unique across the whole
city, mapped to a district. The deed is **account-level, so it survives death**: your heir inherits
it, and its record comes with it.

**The record is the value.** A deed accrues a permanent history of real play — when it was claimed,
every time a bloodline fell holding it, every time it changed hands. That legend is unforgeable and
unfarmable, because it is a record of things that actually happened. It is what makes one street
worth more than another on the market.

**And the split is the whole design: the deed is permanent property; control is earned.** Whoever
controls a corner collects its take — $2,000 an hour, capped at a day's worth — and enjoys that
district's turf perk personally. A rival at level 8 can **muscle in**: a stat contest, and a win
seizes control for twelve hours. You still own the street. You just don't collect on it. Take it back
the same way.

**There's a market.** List your street for cash, floor $10,000. A buyer takes the deed **and the whole
provenance** — but **control resets**, so the buyer inherits the paper and the legend and has to earn
the corner themselves. And a deed can be taken **on-chain**, which needs a made account, a linked
wallet, an unlisted deed and an explicit attestation — after which it is a tradeable NFT that can be
burned to bring the street home, to whoever burned it.

**The map grows.** Neighbourhoods open as the living population crosses thresholds, so late joiners
get fresh ground rather than scraps. Claim before one opens and you hold **frontier** — ground taken
before it was even a neighbourhood.

### The thread

> 1/ Claim a street. Name it yourself. Unique across the entire city, forever.
>
> 2/ It's account-level, so it survives your death. Your heir inherits the paper.
>
> 3/ And the record. Every time it changed hands. Every time a bloodline fell holding it. That
> history is what makes one street worth more than another.
>
> 4/ But owning it and collecting on it are different things. A rival can muscle in and take the
> corner for twelve hours. You still own the street.
>
> 5/ Sell it and the buyer gets the deed and the whole provenance — but control resets. They earn
> the corner themselves.
>
> 6/ Or take it on-chain as a tradeable NFT. Burn it later and the street comes home.
>
> 7/ The map grows as the population does. Claim early enough and your deed says "frontier" — ground
> you took before it was a neighbourhood.

### Headlines
- *You own the paper. They're collecting the rent.*
- *The provenance is the product.*
- *Claimed before it was even a neighbourhood.*

## What the art has to carry

Same rules as the other packs, restated because they bind here too.

- **Fictional only.** No real brand, no real person, no real place. Every name in these chapters is
  invented and the art must stay that way.
- **Period.** 1940s noir. Nothing modern in frame — no screens, no plastic, no contemporary
  typography.
- **No money shots.** Never a wallet balance, never a price chart, never a token logo on a stack of
  cash. The Vice chapter especially: a table, a hand of cards, a race card — never a payout.
- **Never a promise.** No art may imply an income, a return, a floor or an appreciation.
- **Faces are invented.** Any person in frame is a character, not a photograph of somebody.
