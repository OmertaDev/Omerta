# OMERTÀ: Trust Is the Game

797-word launch narration. 6:30 timeline. Final voice: previously selected Voice A. The animatic uses labeled offline scratch speech.

## 00:00–00:15 · The wrong person

The stake is down. One seat is empty. Who do you trust to fill it? This is OMERTÀ: a multiplayer mafia strategy RPG, played in your browser.

## 00:15–00:50 · Nothing to your name

Meet Nico. One name, a little cash, no reputation. Nico's story starts with one simple question: what's worth risking? His first job is small. The cost is nerve; success earns game cash and respect. Now there's a choice: train, bank some money, or risk another job. Training spends energy to improve his stats. Even a bank deposit spends time in vulnerable transit. Progress is deciding what to put at risk next.

## 00:50–01:35 · Pick your way up

Eventually, Nico earns a Path. The Gun favors street combat. The Ledger builds income through fronts and trade. The Kitchen improves cook quality. The Wheel moves convoys faster. The Shadow shortens searches. The Ring improves duels and bouts. Each edge brings a drawback. Nico chooses the Wheel. He likes routes, engines, and arriving before somebody changes their mind. Mara chooses the Gun. Their Paths don't magically solve a heist; the job still checks their preparation and abilities. But they've found different reasons to log in, and different things to bring to the table.

## 01:35–02:15 · More than a menu

Routes connect more than places. A business earns, but upkeep and exposure eat into the plan. A Kitchen batch takes time. Goods occupy cargo space. Cars can be repaired, sold, or broken down; boats put freight on vulnerable water. A price is only useful if you can get the goods there. For Nico, transport becomes a business decision, not background scenery. Around that work: races, boxing, the Den, a Speakeasy, loans, auctions, fixers. Events and NPC activity change the surroundings. The next opportunity may be across town.

## 02:15–03:00 · Alone, you have limits

Nico forms a Crew: a small circle for mutual aid. A Family is a larger structure, with hierarchy, a treasury, rivals, and territory. They're different commitments. For this heist, a temporary team fills two seats: wheelman and muscle. Nico puts up the stake. Mara joins. Preparation improves the odds; it doesn't erase the risk. Families contest districts and commit resources to wars. Convoys move through dangerous routes. Commission seats bring votes on specific city rules. The game can enforce a stake or a role. A promise between friends is still a promise.

## 03:00–03:40 · The city has secrets

A wreck becomes salvage. Salvage becomes material. An eligible recipe turns material into something useful, with a traceable history. That's one part of the World Graph: the city keeps connections that matter. In this city, knowledge has permissions. Discoveries aren't automatically shared with everyone. Two investigators can need independently obtained evidence, not two copies of one clue. Advanced coordination adds contributions and approval; it isn't ordinary Crew chat. The Living World Director selects authored situations from game state. Every discovery can change the next move.

## 03:40–04:10 · Not every player is human

Nico's neighbor might be a person. Or a program operated by one. Here, a sample agent reads an allowed action, submits it, and receives changed game state. The result changes what happens next. An agent makes decisions through the same action-and-result loop. Supported automation has limits: permissions, resources, risk, and bounded actions. NPCs are a separate part of the city.

## 04:10–05:35 · Follow the money

To follow the money, separate three balances. Game cash pays for city life. In-game OMR buys selected equipment, access, and services; some privileges require holding or staking it. Wallet OMR lives onchain. The funded Window spends OMR for game cash. Cash doesn't convert back. Limited game rewards aren't automatic wallet payouts. Many spends recycle OMR; they don't destroy token supply.

The market adds another layer. Trading fees fund separate developer, community, real-world asset, and liquidity budgets. Protocol-owned liquidity remains protocol-controlled. Inventory bonds sell existing OMR released over time. Liquidity rewards need qualifying positions and funded campaigns. Market rewards depend on qualification and available funding. Successful arbitrage can contribute a share of realized profit to reserves. Families compete for designated trading-fee rights, not the pool's principal.

Real-world assets are interests tied to assets outside the game. The system includes Stock Tokens and Commission choices. Allocating a fee is the first step: acquisition, custody, eligibility, and delivery each have their place. Purchase, custody and delivery follow their own rules. Holding OMR does not promise everyone a payout.

## 05:35–06:05 · Power has a cost

Back at the heist, somebody talked. The result doesn't name them. Everyone goes to jail. A character can die; the account continues, with only specified legacy preserved. An heir doesn't recover everything. Seasons have separate rules. Eligible items can be extracted. Exported gear and vehicles stop working in-game. Combat cannot seize unrelated external wallet holdings. You can buy equipment. You cannot buy trust.
